# HOW LONG IS NOW?

*An interactive experiment in clock time, lived time, and remembered time.*

## The premise

Charles and Ray Eames' *Powers of Ten* (1977) begins at a lakeside picnic,
pulls outward by powers of ten until the universe dissolves into scale, then
reverses and dives into the microscopic. It answers **"how big is the
universe?"** by making scale something you travel through.

This project is its psychological-time cousin. It asks:

> **How long is a moment?**

And the answer keeps changing depending on where you're looking from.

The twist that keeps it from being "Powers of Ten but with seconds" is that
the piece has **two dimensions**, not one:

1. **Scale** — a second → a minute → an hour → a day → a year → a decade → a
   lifetime.
2. **Consciousness** — how that same span *feels while you're inside it*
   versus how large it becomes *in memory*.

The second dimension is the project. There is real psychology underneath it:
research distinguishes **prospective time** (duration sensed while something
is happening, dominated by attention) from **retrospective time**
(reconstructed afterward from memory, dominated by contextual change and
remembered events). That is why boredom feels interminable while you sit in
it, yet a repetitive month nearly vanishes when you look back — and why a
vivid week can feel short in the living and enormous in the remembering.

The website makes you *experience* that inversion rather than read about it.

## The experience

No homepage. No hero, no cards, no footer. **You arrive inside a moment.**

Full screen: an ordinary park beside a lake. Grass moving. Someone on a
bench. A bicycle passes. Water, wind, distant conversation. It's 4:17 on an
August afternoon. In the corner:

```
1 SECOND
Scroll to leave now.
```

### The outward journey

- **1 SECOND — NOW.** Almost nothing happens. A leaf moves. Someone blinks.
  A sound crosses the stereo field. The site forces you to *inhabit* the
  second before it lets you skip it.
- **10 SECONDS → 1 MINUTE → 10 MINUTES.** Scroll accelerates the
  *environment*, not a page. People cross the frame. Clouds shift. Shadows
  crawl. A tiny fixed clock stays ruthlessly regular while everything around
  it turns fluid.
- **1 HOUR → 1 DAY → 1 WEEK → 1 YEAR.** The camera begins to rise. The sun
  arcs. Day becomes night. Weather passes through. Trees bloom, fill, turn,
  empty, return. Paths appear in snow. The place becomes a time-lapse
  organism — evoking *Powers of Ten* without copying its literal spatial
  zoom.
- **10 YEARS → A LIFETIME.** People stop being continuous characters and
  become *appearances*. Children grow. Buildings change. Trees become
  enormous. The starting afternoon becomes one infinitesimal coordinate
  buried inside an enormous quantity of lived experience.

### The turn

Everything stops. Black screen. One line:

> *But that isn't how you experienced it.*

And the camera falls — violently or very gently — all the way back into the
original afternoon.

### The inward journey

Now the other direction. Ten minutes → one minute → ten seconds → one second
→ fractions of a second. Instead of atoms, we descend into **attention**: a
glance, a breath, a fragment of conversation, an eye movement, a remembered
association, a sound suddenly noticed. The park resolves into layers of
sensory information. The world is not getting physically smaller — we are
resolving experience more finely.

### The reveal — two clocks (the centerpiece)

Two identical corridors, both labeled **10 MINUTES**.

- In one, someone is waiting for something they desperately want to end.
  The corridor seems endless.
- In the other, they're completely absorbed. The corridor races past.

Then the label changes from **WHILE IT WAS HAPPENING** to **LOOKING BACK**
— and the relationship reverses. The empty experience collapses almost to
nothing. The rich one opens into dozens of individual memory fragments.

Same ten minutes. Different time.

### Memory as a spatial material

A 30-day translucent corridor floats in darkness. **Every day is a pane of
glass.**

A repetitive month produces almost identical panes; pulled backward, they
align perfectly and visually collapse into what looks like one thin sheet.

Then another month: new place, unexpected conversation, difficult
experience, beautiful walk, first attempt at something, wrong turn, new
person, strange weather. Those panes don't align — they scatter through
three-dimensional space like hundreds of photographic slides. The same
thirty-day distance suddenly appears enormous.

No infographic says NOVELTY = MORE MEMORIES. You experience the
compression.

### The ending

Return, one final time, to exactly the same lake. Same ordinary afternoon.
But things that were background are now subtly visible: a shadow moves, a
cloud reforms, a beetle crosses a leaf, someone laughs far away, water hits
the shore.

> *The moment didn't get longer.*
> *You got closer.*

Then the interface disappears. The science connects to attention without a
preachy "live every moment" message.

## The scenes

The experience is driven by a manifest of ten scenes, each owning its
camera, duration, text, assets, audio state, shader state and transition:

| # | Scene | Beat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 01 | `now` | The park. One second. Inhabit it. |
| 02 | `ten-minutes` | Environment accelerates; the fixed clock appears. |
| 03 | `day` | Sun arcs, light rotates, the first rise. |
| 04 | `year` | Seasons as one continuous organism. |
| 05 | `lifetime` | People become appearances; the afternoon becomes a coordinate. |
| 06 | `return` | Black. "But that isn't how you experienced it." The fall. |
| 07 | `attention` | The inward zoom — resolving experience more finely. |
| 08 | `two-clocks` | The prospective/retrospective reversal. Centerpiece. |
| 09 | `memory-compression` | Thirty days as panes of glass. |
| 10 | `return-to-now` | The same lake, seen closer. The interface disappears. |

## Sound

Sound matters almost as much as visuals. One environmental soundscape,
transformed as time changes: birds become dense textures, footsteps become
pulses, years collapse into noise — and the inward zoom isolates individual
sounds again. Everything gets a visible sound on/off control.

## What done looks like first

Not the website. Three finished moments:

1. **The park → one year transition.**
2. **The identical ten minutes, lived vs. remembered.**
3. **The thirty-day memory-compression corridor.**

If those three are beautiful, responsive, and intellectually legible with
almost no explanatory copy, the project exists. If they aren't, we haven't
spent three weeks building the universe yet.
